Short ads get most of the attention, and for good reason. A 15-second UGC ad is cheap to make, easy to test and quick to fatigue-check. For a lot of ecommerce products it is the right length.

Some products need more room. If a viewer needs to understand how something works before they will click, a 15-second ad either rushes the explanation or skips it. Courses, software, finance education, high-ticket items and anything with a learning curve often do better with 45 to 90 seconds of clear explanation.

This post covers three things: when a long ad is worth testing, how to script one so it holds attention, and how Vooads builds long videos out of short parts.

## When long ads are worth testing

A long ad is a bet that the extra seconds earn their keep. They earn it when the viewer has a real question that the ad answers.

Test a long ad when:

- **The product needs a demo with steps.** Software, apps with a setup flow, kitchen gadgets that do several things.
- **The price is high enough that people need reasons.** Furniture, courses, coaching, property.
- **The main objection needs time to answer.** "Is this safe?", "Will it work for me?", "How is this different?"
- **You are on YouTube Shorts.** Shorts viewers tend to be more patient with explainers than Reels viewers. Treat that as a hypothesis to test in your own account.
- **Your short ad has a high hold rate but a low click rate.** People are interested and unconvinced. More explanation may help.

Skip the long ad when:

- The product is simple and visual. A phone case does not need 60 seconds.
- Your short ads are losing people in the first 3 seconds. Length will not fix a weak hook.
- You have not found a winning angle yet. Use short ads to find the angle. Use long ads to scale it.

A good habit: when a 15 or 30-second ad wins, cut a 45 or 60-second version from the same angle and test it next to the original. Let the data decide.

## How long ads lose people

Long ads fail in predictable ways. Knowing them makes scripting easier.

1. **The slow start.** The script spends 10 seconds on background before saying anything the viewer cares about. The hook still has to land in 3 seconds, whatever the length.
2. **The flat middle.** Between second 20 and 40 nothing changes. Same tone, same framing, same rhythm. People drift.
3. **The list without a story.** Six features read out in a row. Each one is fine. Together they are a brochure.
4. **The buried CTA.** The viewer who made it to second 55 is your hottest prospect. If the CTA is vague, they leave.

Every rule in the next section fixes one of these.

## Scripting a 45 to 90 second UGC ad

### Keep the hook short and early

The first line carries the same weight as in a 15-second ad. Twelve words or fewer, spoken in the first 3 seconds, and the same idea on screen as text for people watching on mute.

### Use a story arc, then a demo

A structure that works for 60 seconds:

| Time | Beat | Example line |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3s | Hook | "I used to spend Friday afternoon on reports." |
| 3 to 10s | Problem with a scene | "Three spreadsheets, two exports, and my weekend started late every week." |
| 10 to 18s | Failed alternatives | "I tried templates. I tried a freelancer. Both broke the moment our numbers changed." |
| 18 to 26s | Discovery and mechanism | "This tool pulls the numbers in for me and builds the report on a schedule." |
| 26 to 38s | Demo, three details | "I connect the account once. I pick the metrics. It sends the report every Friday at four." |
| 38 to 46s | Proof device | "Here is last week's report. You can see exactly where each number comes from." |
| 46 to 53s | Top objection | "And if you are worried about access, it only reads the data. It cannot change anything." |
| 53 to 60s | CTA and recap | "Reports done, Friday back. Start the free trial at the link." |

For 90 seconds, add a second use case after the demo ("It also works for client reports") and answer two objections instead of one.

Every claim in that table would need to be true for the real product. If the product does not have read-only access, that line goes.

### Add a pattern interrupt every 6 to 10 seconds

A pattern interrupt is anything that resets attention: a question to the viewer, a turn ("Here is the part nobody mentions"), a count ("Second thing"), a close-up line ("Look at this"). In a long ad you need several. Plan them into the script; do not hope the edit adds them.

### Write one thought per line

This matters more for long videos than for short ones, and the reason is how they are made. More on that below. For now: keep each spoken line to about 25 English words or fewer, and finish the thought inside the line.

### End with a clear CTA and a reason

Tell the viewer exactly what happens after the tap. "Start the free trial. It takes a minute and you do not need a card" is clearer than "Check it out". Only use offers and conditions that are real.

## Pacing: how many words fit

A natural ad read in English runs at about 2.4 words per second. Take away about 10% for pauses and the CTA, and you get rough budgets:

- 30 seconds: about 70 to 80 words
- 45 seconds: about 105 to 115 words
- 60 seconds: about 140 to 150 words
- 90 seconds: about 210 to 225 words

Other languages differ. Spanish and Portuguese translations usually run 15 to 25% longer than English, so rewrite shorter instead of reading faster. German and Russian carry longer words, so fewer fit per second. Mandarin, Japanese and Korean are counted in characters or syllable blocks. The Vooads script writer uses these budgets automatically when it writes for a length and language.

If the script is over budget, cut a beat. A presenter who speeds up to fit sounds like a disclaimer.

## How Vooads builds long videos

AI presenter models render short clips well. Most of them have a maximum length per render, often around 10 seconds. A 60-second ad from a single render is either impossible or unreliable with most of them.

So Vooads renders long videos in parts and joins them. Here is what happens when you ask for a 60-second UGC video:

1. **The script is split into slices.** Each slice is about 10 seconds of speech. Vooads splits on line boundaries, which is why one thought per line matters.
2. **Each slice is rendered in order.** Same presenter, same voice, same framing. The studio shows the plan before you start: "60s video = 6 parts of 10s, stitched automatically."
3. **The parts are stitched.** Vooads joins the clips into one file, in order.
4. **You get one video in your library.** Ready to download like any other ad.

If the stitching step is not available on a server, the job still finishes and you get the parts as a playlist in order, with a note. You can join them in any editor.

### What this means for your script

- **End lines on complete thoughts.** A sentence that runs across a cut can sound slightly off at the join. Short, complete lines hide the joins.
- **Keep tone steady.** A big shift in energy between two lines can look like a jump at the seam. If you want a tone shift, make it a short line of its own ("Okay. Here is the thing.").
- **Avoid long pauses at line ends.** Let the next line start the next idea.
- **Watch the joins once before launch.** If one feels abrupt, trim it in your editor or render that length again.

## A testing plan for long ads

Here is a simple way to find out whether long ads work for your product:

1. **Start with a short winner.** Pick a 15 or 30-second ad that is holding up.
2. **Write the long version on the same angle.** Same hook, same promise. Add the failed alternatives, a fuller demo, and one objection.
3. **Run them side by side.** Same ad set or a clean split. Same audience, same budget.
4. **Compare cost per result,** with hold rate and click rate as supporting signals. Give it enough spend to be sure.
5. **Cut a middle length.** If 60 beats 15, try 30 or 45. Often there is a sweet spot.
6. **Localise the winner.** A long ad that wins in one market is a strong candidate for the next. Rewrite the script natively, check the word budget for the new language, and pick a presenter who fits.

## Compliance in long ads

More seconds means more chances to slip in a risky line. The usual rules apply all the way through:

- No income claims or guaranteed returns in finance and trading ads. Include a clear risk line.
- No health or medical claims, and no before/after in health, weight and skincare.
- No lines that assert something personal about the viewer, such as their weight, money troubles or a condition.
- No invented testimonials. An AI presenter can share a point of view. It should not claim a history it does not have.

## Quick checklist

- The product needs explanation, and a short ad already shows interest.
- Hook in the first 3 seconds, 12 words or fewer, on screen as text.
- Story arc first, then the demo.
- A pattern interrupt every 6 to 10 seconds.
- One complete thought per line, about 25 words or fewer.
- Words fit the budget for the length and language.
- Clear CTA that says what happens after the tap.
- Watch the joins once, with sound and on mute.
- Test it against the short version before you scale it.

Long ads are a tool for a specific job. When a product needs explaining and the viewer is already interested, the extra seconds can do work a short ad cannot. Build them carefully, test them against your short winners, and keep the ones your data supports.
